Introduction

Why homeownership slipped out of reach for a generation

Research Topic & Significance

Owning homes has been one of the largest most defining components of wealth for most households. The age at which one buys their first home shapes how much wealth will potentially accumulate depending on size, location etc. Homes that could have been bought by someone in their mid 20s to mid 30s 2 decades ago is now steadily moving out of reach for people now in their mid 20s to 30s. Census housing vacancy surveys show that among households aged 25-29 years dropped 41.8% in 2006 to 30.9% in 2016, recovering only to 33.3 percent by 2025. 30-34 year old home owners followed the same path, from 57.4 percent down to 45.4 percent and back to 46.6 percent. Neither group has returned to where it stood before the 2008 financial crisis, more than fifteen years later. Across the same period the typical American home rose in value from roughly $132,000 to $370,000.

The most well-known explanation for this is that wages failed to keep up with prices of homes. Median earnings of a full-time worker aged 25-34 rose from $28,561 to $59,176 between 2000 and 2025, which looks like a doubling of purchasing power. Adjusted for inflation, the same earnings move from $53,403 to $55,435 between 2000 and 2019. A gain of under four percent across nineteen years, before rising to $59,176 by 2025. A young worker's paycheck was therefore close to flat for almost 2 decades while the price of homes that workers are trying to buy has almost tripled. Ratios tell the same story from another angle: the price of a typical home rose from 4.6 times a young worker's annual earnings in 2000 to 6.3 times in 2025.

Stakeholders

The group that is directly affected is one in three Americans aged 25-34 who rent and would prefer to own homes. The gap between renting and owning determines whether monthly housing costs build equity or disappear. This delay compounds since a purchase postponed by 10 years is 10 years of equity never accumulated. Families are increasingly supplying down-payment assistance that turns a market outcome into an inherited one which widens the distance between households that can offer such help and those that cannot.

Lenders and homebuilders have a commercial stake in this since first time buyers are the entry point to the housing market and shrinking cohort of them constrains transaction volume throughout the chain. Municipal and state governments have a fiscal and practical stake. Property taxes fund local services and metropolitan area where young workers cannot afford to buy face difficulty retaining teachers, nurses and other essential workers. Housing policy makers need to know whether the constraint is price, income, credit cost, or supply, because each points to a different intervention: zoning reform, down-payment assistance, interest-rate policy, or construction subsidy.

Existing Solutions & Gaps

The tools for measuring housing affordability are readily available and mainly address a single issue. The mortgage calculator available from Zillow, NerdWallet, and virtually all retail banks informs a particular household of its affordability at current interest rates, based on current income. They are useful to make a decision but not so much to capture a trend. They describe one household at one moment and fail to say anything about how the situation has changed or how it differs between places. Industry reporting does fill parts of that gap. The National Association of Realtors publishes their annual Buyer-Seller Profile while Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies publishes their “State of the Nation's Housing” reports that look at first-time buyer age and homeownership trends over the years.

Research on why young-adult homeownership fell has concentrated on demographic explanation. A joint center analysis attributed part of the decline to later marriage, changing household composition and migration toward central cities, but concluded that demographic factors account for only about a quarter of the variation in whether a household owns or rents, leaving macroeconomic conditions and price as the larger share of the explanation.

That leaves 2 gaps in this project to address. The first is that most public treatments measure affordability as a price-to-income ratio and ignore the cost of borrowing entirely which is a serious omission. In 2021 the price-to-income ratio stood near a record 6.6 while the share of income required to service a mortgage was a moderate 26.5 percent, because money cost 2.96 percent; by 2023 that share reached 41.5 percent, worse than the 2006 housing-bubble peak of 39.7 percent, on a price-to-income ratio that had barely moved. Secondly, the national aggregates mask the geography almost entirely. In 2025 the price of a typical home ranged from 0.81 times a young worker's annual earnings in Helena, Arkansas to 27.1 times in San Jose, California, a spread across 894 metropolitan areas that no national average can represent.

Dataset Considerations

Three sources were selected, each covering something the others cannot. The project is centered on three sources: the Census Housing Vacancy Survey for homeownership by age of householder, the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis FRED API for earnings, mortgage rates, prices and inflation, and the Zillow Home Value Index for home values at metropolitan level. Cleaning reshapes each into tidy tables, which is non-trivial because none of the three arrives in an analysable form. The Census workbook repeats an entire year-block down the sheet, the FRED series arrive at three different frequencies, and the Zillow file is 319 columns wide. Dependent variable, the homeownership by age of householder is provided by the Census Housing Vacancy Survey starting in 1982 and using age categories fine enough to distinguish between those aged 25 to 29 and those aged 30 to 34. This is important since the two age categories are on either side of the normal first purchase and data sources using a broader “under 35” category lump them together. Economic variables include the median weekly earnings by age category, the 30-year fixed mortgage rate, national homeownership rate, median sales price, the Case–Shiller index, the Consumer Price Index and housing starts and are obtained through the FRED API. Getting the data programmatically from an API rather than downloaded ensures reproducibility and enabled inclusion of the mortgage rate and inflation data that are key to the affordability analysis. The geographical component of the data comes from the Zillow Home Value Index that reports a median home value in 894 metro areas monthly since 2000.

Several limitations follow from these choices and are carried through the analysis. None of the three sources is individual-level, so the project describes aggregate rates rather than the circumstances of particular households. Earnings are reported nationally rather than by metropolitan area, which means metro-level affordability measures compare local prices against a national income and cannot capture the fact that salaries in San Jose differ from salaries in Cleveland. The Zillow index covers the middle tier of the market, between the 33rd and 67th percentiles of value, and therefore excludes both the cheapest and most expensive segments. Homeownership figures are reported for the age of the householder rather than of every occupant, so a 27-year-old living in a parent's home is counted under that parent's age. Each of these is a genuine constraint on what the results can support, and each is noted again where it bears on a specific finding.

Next comes feature engineering, which generates the variables used in the analysis: price-to-income ratio, number of years needed to accumulate savings for a down-payment, mortgage payment per month, and the payment ratio relative to income, alongside measures of growth, volatility, and ranking for each metro area.

The modelling stage answers the research questions posed above in two classes. Clustering and principal component analysis, unsupervised models, seek whether there are types of housing market, and how they differ. Naive Bayes, decision trees, support vector machines, regression and neural networks, supervised models, seek whether the affordability of a market can be predicted by its pricing characteristics, and which variables contain that information. Each model is described in turn, outlining its underlying assumptions, parameter space, and limitations on what its findings mean.

Research Questions

Ten questions this project sets out to answer, and the section of this site where each is addressed.

  1. How much has the likelihood of owning a home changed for Americans in their twenties and early thirties over the past two decades?
  2. Have the earnings of young workers kept pace with the price of the homes they would need to buy?
  3. How many years of saving does a typical young worker now need to reach a twenty percent down payment, and how does that compare with someone the same age in 2000?
  4. How has homeownership among householders under 25 differed from those aged 25 to 34, and what might explain the divergence?
  5. How much does a young person's chance of owning depend on which metropolitan area they live in?
  6. Has the distance between the most and least affordable housing markets widened since 2000?
  7. Do American housing markets fall into distinct types that present different obstacles to young buyers?
  8. Which characteristics most separate an affordable metropolitan area from an unaffordable one?
  9. Can a metropolitan area's affordability be anticipated from its recent price behaviour alone?
  10. Did the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic leave lasting marks on young-adult homeownership, or did the market return to its earlier path?
